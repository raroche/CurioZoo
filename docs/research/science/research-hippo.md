# Hippo Pond: float-or-sink research

Research for **Hippo Pond**, the float-or-sink game in the CurioZoo science room (ages 5–13, EN/ES, offline).
Compiled 2026-10-09. Each fact has a source tag `[Sn]`; the URLs are listed in **Sources** at the end.

**Ground rules this file follows**

- A verdict is only given when a source states it, or when it follows directly from sourced densities with a clear margin.
- `A` means **AVOID** for scored predictions. Use it when the density is within about ±3% of the liquid, when sources disagree, or when the result depends on brand, ripeness or how the object was made.
- `?` means there is no reliable data. Do not score it.
- `est.` marks a typical mass that is the author's estimate. It is fine for "big vs small" art, but it is not a measured fact.
- **One inference rule is used throughout.** If an object floats in fresh water (density < 1.00), it also floats in sea water, brine and honey, because all three are denser than water. If an object sinks in honey (density > ~1.45), it sinks in every liquid in this file. Only the vegetable-oil column (0.91–0.92) needs its own density number.

### Density column: what is stable and safe to show

- **Default 3-layer pond (recommended):** honey (1.38–1.45) / water (1.00) / vegetable oil (0.92). The gaps between layers are large. Oil and water do not mix [S4], and honey sinks below water. The densities come from [S3], [S4] and [S39].
- **The full 7-layer column works in real life:** honey / corn syrup / dish soap / water / oil / rubbing alcohol / lamp oil [S4]. It needs slow pouring, and Spangler notes that the measured densities do not always match the layer order, because of brand variation [S4]. For a kids' game, show at most 4–5 layers and skip lamp oil and alcohol (both flammable [S4]).
- **Do not put these next to each other** (their densities overlap or nearly touch): corn syrup with maple syrup, milk with dish soap, honey with dark corn syrup (1.37 vs 1.38+).
- **Where objects stop in the 3-layer pond** (worked out from the densities in the catalogue below):

| Stops at… | Objects (density) |
|---|---|
| Floating on top of the oil | cork (0.20–0.25), balsa (0.11–0.14), pine wood/pencil (0.35–0.57), styrofoam (0.015–0.03), tennis ball (≈0.33–0.41), ping-pong ball (≈0.08), butter (0.86–0.87, margin small) |
| Oil/water boundary (sinks in oil, floats in water) | **beeswax** (0.96), **HDPE plastic, e.g. a milk-jug piece** (0.96). Ice (0.917) is AVOID here because it "floats (just)" on oil [S5]. |
| Water/honey boundary (sinks in water, floats on honey) | grape, potato (1.06–1.10), fresh egg, golf ball (≤1.13), nylon (1.12–1.17), acrylic (1.19), ebony and lignum vitae (≤1.33, small margin) |
| Bottom | coin, key, paper clip, marble, pebble, bone (1.7–2.0), chalk, clay, tight foil ball, 16-lb bowling ball (≈1.33 vs honey ≥1.38: AVOID, margin too small) |

| Hippos can't swim and can't even float. Their bodies are too dense. ### Wording pitfalls teachers warn about

| Pitfall | Why it's a problem | Say instead | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| "Heavy things sink, light things float." | This is the core misconception. Children use a weight rule until a counterexample breaks it. | "heavy/light **for its size**" | [S50], [S51], [S52], [S53] |
| Overcorrecting to "big things sink" or "size matters" | It is the mass-to-volume ratio that matters, not size. | Always pair a big floater with a big sinker (log vs anchor) and a small floater with a small sinker (cork vs coin). | [S54] |
| "If it's heavy enough it will sink" (more of the same stuff) | More bricks means 10× the weight but also 10× the volume, so the block is no heavier for its size. | Show big and small pieces of the same material behaving alike. | [S51] |
| "Air makes it float" / "it floats because it has air in it" | This is a documented misconception stage. Solid wax, wood, ice and butter float with no air pockets. | "Air takes up room without adding much weight", and only use it for hollow things (boat, peel, pepper, ball). | [S52], [S53] (Moore & Harrison 2007) |
| "Floating means part of it sticks out; under the water means sinking." | Children treat a fully submerged hovering object as sinking. | Show "hovering" (an egg in the middle of layered salt water, a fish) as its own state. | [S53] (Joung 2009); [S52] |
| "Molecules are closer together" (one extension handout explains fruit this way) | For fruit and vegetables the difference is mostly **air spaces** (apples have up to ~30% air by volume). Particle talk is beyond ages 5–8. | "It has lots of tiny air spaces inside." | [S10] vs [S16] |
| "Salt water is heavier." | Too loose: a small cup of salt water weighs less than a big bucket of fresh water. | "Salty water is heavier **for its size**." | [S51] (same framing) |
| "Sugar is heavy" (soda cans) | An oversimplification. What matters is how much is dissolved in the same can. | "The regular can has lots of sugar packed into the same can, so it's heavier for its size." | [S7] |
| A paper clip resting on the surface | That is surface tension, not floating, and it breaks if poked. | Always drop it in. | [S35] |
| Making kids calculate density | It adds cognitive load and is not needed. | Qualitative comparisons up to about age 10. Show the fraction-under rule only in older-kid levels. | [S51] |

## 5. Research on changing children's ideas

- **Smith, Carey & Wiser (1985), *Cognition* 21:177–237.** This study tested 78 children aged 3–9. Young children's "weight" is *felt weight*, and density is absent from their thinking. Weight and density differentiate gradually with age [S56]. Later work found some intuitive density competence even in preschoolers [S56].
- **TERC Inquiry Project.** At the start of Grade 3, children confuse weight and density. After a weight → volume → material curriculum, nearly three-quarters of Inquiry students showed some density insight by the end of Grade 4, compared with fewer than half of the control students [S55].
- **Hardy, Jonen, Möller & Stern (2006), *J. Educ. Psych.* 98:307–326.** This study followed 161 third graders: one group with high instructional support, one with low support (each in 3 classes), and a baseline group, with tests before, after and one year later. **One year later the high-support group was ahead** on reducing misconceptions and using scientific explanations [S57]. The unit was long, 11 × 45 min [S60].

  The following details are recalled from the paper and **not verified here**; check the full text before relying on them. High support meant sequencing the topic into sub-steps (material kind → water displacement → density/buoyancy) and teacher scaffolding such as highlighting contradictions.
- **Leuchter, Saalbach & Hardy (2014), *IJSE*.** This study gave sequenced material to 244 children in 15 classes in the first school years. It found fewer misconceptions, more correct float/sink predictions than a comparison group, and a more developed idea of **material kind** [S58].
- **Hardy, Saalbach, Leuchter & Schalk (2020), *Frontiers in Psychology*.** This study tested 4–7-year-olds. Showing **two same-material "standards"** that behave the same way improved material-kind predictions, though the effect was small. Verbal labels helped inconsistently, and transfer to new objects was weak [S59].
- **Schwichow & Zoupidis (2024) meta-analysis.** It covered 69 studies and 191 effect sizes, with a mean g = 0.85. Teaching floating and sinking works even in elementary school, and age was unrelated to the effect. It needs **multiple hours**, and **hands-on beats virtual experiments**, which the authors credit to haptic feedback [S60].
- **Typical progression** (Open University). Children move from "heavy sinks / light floats" → "air makes it float" → "light for size, and shape matters". Children also explain two similar objects inconsistently [S52].

**Design implications**

1. Start with **material kind**: several sizes of the same material behave alike [S58], [S59], [S51].
2. Then contrast big-light with small-heavy.
3. Then cover shape and hollowness.
4. Then change the liquid (salt or layers).
5. Then cover "how deep" for older players.
6. Ask for a prediction *before* every drop [S52].
7. Since virtual work is weaker than hands-on [S60], add "try it at home/class" prompts with safe kitchen items (cork, coin, apple, grape, egg + salt, clay, foil).

**"Make it float" mechanic.** For an object tied to a float, the combined density is (m₁ + m₂) ÷ (V₁ + V₂). Example (est.): a ~6 g key (~0.7 cm³) plus a ~4 g cork (~17 cm³ at 0.23) gives about 0.56, so it floats. The formula is standard Archimedes [S37]; the masses are estimates.
